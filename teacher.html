TEACHER GUIDE / CASE 01

The Harbor Hall Tip

A fictional newsroom exercise for upper-secondary media literacy, civics, or IB Theory of Knowledge. It is formative practice, not a mark or an efficacy claim.

Run it in 25 minutes

  1. 3 min · Brief. Ask: “When two posts say the same thing, what would make them independent?”
  2. 12–15 min · Play. Students play as desk editor; work alone or in pairs. Ask them to keep one reason for a decision, one thing they could not confirm, and one question for discussion.
  3. 7 min · Debrief. Compare the cost of publishing too soon with the cost of withholding a useful, already-supported detail. Discuss why a visible correction helps but does not erase consequences.

Open index.html from the folder or a school-approved static host. There are no accounts, time limits, external feeds, saved student responses or submissions. Reloading resets a run. No party, political position, or real person is assessed.

What to look for

  • Origins: the circulating post is a crop of the clerk’s notice, not independent corroboration. The library duty lead observed preparations independently.
  • Scope: an old door being locked verifies that door, not cancellation. The move and time are supported; intent is unresolved.
  • Editorial responsibility: an accurate notice can publish practical details and disclose limits. Holding avoids a false alert but has a public cost. If the newsroom gets it wrong, correct the original visibly.

Discussion and evidence of learning

  1. Which detail does each source actually support? Which claim would another eyewitness not be able to settle?
  2. What should an honest correction say that a silent edit would not?
  3. For TOK: how much warrant is enough before a knower makes a public claim? Does the cost of being wrong change the needed standard of evidence?

Quick transfer check: the +60% chart measures two opening Saturdays. Calling it an annual chart is misleading; the true annual change remains unresolved. The independent tally supports 128 for this day, but not a causal effect of free entry. Ask students to name a source that could help with each question.

Next lesson: offer a new, balanced claim without the game labels. Ask independently for a verdict, the evidence, and how confident they are. Don’t infer learning gains from finishing the episode.
